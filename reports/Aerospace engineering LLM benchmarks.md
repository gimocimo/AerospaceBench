# Aerospace benchmarks measure recall, not engineering

No published benchmark yet measures how well frontier LLMs or LLM agents perform demanding aerospace engineering work, and the gap is widest exactly where engineering judgement lives. About two dozen aerospace evaluations exist, almost all released between mid-2025 and October 2026, and most are recognition-style question sets over aviation operations, maintenance and regulations, where frontier models score 60–83% (GPT-5.5 reaches 82.7% on Pre-Flight against an informal ~95% expert reference). A thin layer of small, single-sector agentic benchmarks with physics or safety verifiers is far more informative and far less saturated: no model produced a valid GTOC-12 mission submission, the best safety-gated cockpit agent succeeds 72.6% of the time but only 53.4% across three consecutive runs, and frontier models score 15–31 against a human expert's 92 on simulated rocket precision landing. Rotorcraft, aerostructures, certification artefacts, test and AIT, orbital launch-vehicle design, spacecraft subsystem budgets and flight-software generation have effectively no LLM evaluation at all. Adjacent fields supply the playbook: general-engineering benchmarks show textbook scores saturating (ThermoQA 94%) while executable design stays near one-third (EngDesign 34%, CADWorld 17.5% versus 87% for experts); engineering-software agent studies show a roughly 50-point gap between a simulation that runs and one that is right; and expert-graded professional benchmarks, together with audits that forced FrontierMath to change 42% of its problems, show that answer-key verification rather than task authorship is the binding constraint. A fully open toolchain (OpenMDAO/Aviary, SU2/OpenFOAM, CalculiX/MYSTRAN, NASA CEA, RocketPy, JSBSim/PX4 SITL, GMAT/Orekit/Basilisk, cFS/F Prime) and CC0 or public reference data (NASA TMR, Drag Prediction Workshop CRM cases, HART II, C-MAPSS, ESA-ADB, EASA CS-25 XML) can now ground most of a pilot, with real holes in rotorcraft comprehensive analysis, damage tolerance, flutter and paywalled DO-178C/ARP standards. The right pilot is therefore a validation study of the instrument itself: roughly 30–60 interview-derived tasks, each blind re-solved by a second engineer and graded by deterministic checks plus a validated expert rubric over at least three runs, whose deliverables are error rates, agreement statistics and variance estimates rather than a leaderboard.

## About two dozen aerospace evaluations exist, and most test recall

The aerospace evaluation literature is young and lopsided. Nearly every substantive benchmark appeared between mid-2025 and October 2026, and the set sorts into three tiers that shrink in size as they grow in authenticity: large recognition-style question sets over aviation operations, maintenance and regulations; small expert-written question-answer or textbook-calculation sets; and a handful of agentic, tool-using tasks graded by physics or safety verifiers. Only the third tier resembles engineering work, and each of its members covers one narrow sector. Model names below are given exactly as the cited papers report them.

### Aviation benchmarks cluster in operations, maintenance and safety text

The most visible aviation benchmark is **Pre-Flight**: 300 five-option multiple-choice questions on airport ground operations and ICAO and FAA rules, written by the lead author with a small group of practitioners and distributed under an MIT licence inside UK AISI's `inspect_evals` ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01829); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/AirsideLabs/pre-flight-06)). Across 44 models, **GPT-5.5 leads at 82.7%** against an informal expert reference of about 95%, up from roughly 75% for early-2025 models. The authors describe their own validation as only partial: no inter-annotator agreement was computed, and answer-key problems surfaced in the FAA category ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01829)). MITRE and the FAA's **ALUE** is a framework more than a fixed test. It is an Apache-2.0 harness for multiple-choice, retrieval, summarisation (scored by LLM-judge claim decomposition), extraction and classification tasks, positioned as a tool for assuring LLMs in aerospace, but it ships with no public leaderboard and no documented dataset provenance ([GitHub](https://github.com/mitre/alue); [MITRE/FAA release](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250917980616/en/MITRE-and-FAA-Introduce-Novel-Aerospace-Large-Language-Model-Evaluation-Benchmark)). The **Civil Aviation Maintenance Benchmark (CAMB)** pairs 7,969 maintenance-licensing questions across 12 aircraft types with 50 real B737/A320 fault-tree cases; the best model, Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct, reaches **68.87%**, and the data is Chinese-language and non-commercially licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20420v1); [GitHub](https://github.com/CamBenchmark/cambenchmark)). The only aerospace manufacturing benchmark, about 7,500 multi-answer questions on assembly and materials, was generated by Gemini from undisclosed textbooks, and only about 300 items were expert-reviewed. Its top score is 50.75% under +1/−3 marking, and aerospace materials questions average just 35.4% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.17183v1)).

Engineering in the design sense barely appears. **AeroEngQA**, the only human-authored aircraft-engineering QA set, holds just 80 context–question–answer items that four aeronautical engineers wrote from NASA and NTSB reports and patents, balanced between answerable and unanswerable questions. Its most useful finding is that models answered the deliberately unanswerable questions from training data instead of abstaining ([Southampton preprint](https://southampton.ac.uk/~sem03/aiaa-2025-preprint.pdf)). A Penn State study of 100 undergraduate aerodynamics and stability exam questions found GPT-4 at **86.5% on Remember-level items but 50% on Apply-level items**, with accuracy falling as Bloom's level rose ([ASEE](https://peer.asee.org/57581.pdf)). One potentially overlapping item remains unread: Brian Connolly's SwRI "Aerospace Engineering Evaluation Set" (AIAA SciTech 2025, paper 2025-0702), described as a multimodal set of aerospace test questions and tasks, whose size, content and scores could not be retrieved ([AIAA](https://arc.aiaa.org/doi/10.2514/6.2025-0702)). It should be read before AerospaceBench makes any novelty claim.

The operations and agentic studies carry the most mature evaluation design. **AeroCopilotBench** runs 73 emergency-procedure tasks for a Cessna 172S and Piper PA-44 in an executable symbolic cockpit and counts success only when every POH goal is met with no hard-constraint violation. GPT-5.6-sol leads at **72.6% safety-gated success but 53.4% pass^3**, and models skip steps in some runs that they perform in others ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.16349)). On 1,000 verified Singapore Changi ground-frequency utterances, a consequence-weighted ATC Risk Score peaks at 0.69 for Gemini-3-flash-preview on clean transcripts and **collapses to 0.00–0.07 under ASR noise** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.11769)). A companion study built with 40 air traffic controllers found that standard semantic metrics overstated operational reliability ([summary](https://aiunderstanding.org/news/preprint-finds-standard-language-model-scores-can-overstate-safety-in-air-traffic)). FLY-EVAL++, which extends PilotBench to 66 models, found safety-score spreads above 28 points among models with similar predictive accuracy ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04021)). The shared lesson is that safety-gated, consequence-weighted and repeated-run scoring exposes far less reliability than plain accuracy does.

### Space, launch, drone and flight-software benchmarks are few, small and verifier-graded

Space engineering has the strongest examples of executable grading. MIT's **APBench** poses 299 open astrodynamics problems, plus a 1,990-item private split withheld for copyright. Answers are numeric or free-form, with deliberately no multiple choice, magnitude-scaled tolerances and an embedded canary string. o1-preview led late-2024 models at 66.6%, while the astronomy-tuned AstroLLaMA scored 1.0%, which shows that domain-science pretraining does not transfer to engineering calculation ([Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11885820/fullTextXML)). The **GTOC-12 agent study** gave agents an open astrodynamics stack (PyKEP, PyGMO, TudatPy, poliastro, SpiceyPy, CasADi, Dymos) and up to 10 hours per attempt to design an asteroid-mining campaign. Outputs were scored on a 26-criterion expert rubric applied by a Gemini 2.5 Pro judge with intra-rater ICC(2,1) of 0.748. Claude Sonnet 4.5 scored 21.15/26 and GPT-5 13.54, but **no model produced a submission that passed the GTOC validator**. Failures were dominated by km/m and day/second unit mixing, boundary-condition errors and blind debugging loops ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.03630)).

**AstroAgentBench** is the closest existing analogue to what AerospaceBench needs. It has 116 satellite-scheduling and constellation-design cases, 35 of them held out, graded by a layered verifier: schema and timeout first, then SGP4 propagation, frame, geometry, power, slew and link constraints, then mission value, all anchored to MILP and constraint-programming baselines. Codex CLI with GPT-5.4 produced 35/35 valid submissions and Claude Code with Claude Opus 4.6 produced 26/35. Agents beat the solver baselines on relay and revisit planning but trailed badly on geometry-heavy stereo imaging (68.91 vs 96.05), and **22.3% of runs committed an artefact before reading the workspace contract** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11354)). On raw telemetry, ATSADBench found LLM multivariate anomaly detection close to random, with retrieval giving no significant benefit ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.12448)).

Launch and drone work is thinner still. **RocketBench** asks models to design high-power rockets simulated in RocketPy. On precision landing, frontier models averaged 15–31 points against a human expert's 91.6, yet a 7B model trained with GRPO over about 3,000 simulations reached 95.6, a warning that simulator-in-the-loop scores move fast once labs train on similar environments. The benchmark itself is unreleased ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.19394v2)). RocketSmith, a Claude Code plugin orchestrating OpenRocket, build123d and PrusaSlicer, flew four printed rockets that reached 80–84% of predicted apogee ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.00097v3)). RocketAgent's liquid thrust-chamber agent was demonstrated on a single case, with proprietary Fluent and ABAQUS checks and no repository ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.06044)). For drones, **DroneServer** grades ten ArduPilot and PX4 SITL missions from telemetry, and eight of eleven model families completed 90–100% of flying missions, so basic command execution is near saturation ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15486v3)). UAVBench's 50,000 multiple-choice questions are LLM-generated ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.11252v1)). Aerial vision-language navigation sets such as AerialVLN, where licensed pilots reach 80.8% against at most 1.6% for baseline models, measure robot policy learning rather than engineering ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.06735v1)).

Flight software has no executable generation benchmark for cFS, F Prime or PX4. RepoSpace reports 825 repository-level samples of spaceborne-equipment code, but its languages, metrics and availability are unverified ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3390/aerospace12060498)). The formal-methods results are telling. LLM translation of aerospace requirements into temporal logic reaches 88.4% semantic accuracy ([Req2LTL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17334)), but end-to-end verification of nine Lockheed Martin cyber-physical systems with Claude 3.5 Sonnet and the ESBMC model checker reaches only **46.5%** ([SpecVerify](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.04857)). A NASA Langley memorandum concludes that much remains to be demonstrated before LLMs can generate or assess assurance arguments in certification practice ([NASA TM-20250001849](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250001849/downloads/NASA-TM-20250001849.pdf)).

## Design, analysis, certification and test remain essentially unmeasured

Mapping the catalogue onto lifecycle stages and vehicle types shows the problem plainly. Every populated cell below is either an operations or maintenance text task, a narrow agentic task, or a demonstration without reproducible scoring. The engineering core of the matrix is empty.

| Domain | Design | Analysis | Test / AIT | Certification / assurance | Manufacturing | Operations & maintenance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed-wing aircraft | AVPD demo (18 tasks, proprietary CAD) | Penn State MCQ (100); AeroEngQA (80) | none | aeroBERT requirement NER and classification | LLM-generated MCQ (~7.5k) | Pre-Flight, ALUE, CAMB, KEO/OMIn, PilotBench, AeroCopilotBench, ATC sets |
| Rotorcraft | none | none | none | none | none | none |
| Aero-engines | TurboAgent demo | none (only RUL prognostics) | none | none | none | CAMB fault cases (partial) |
| Avionics, FCS, flight software | none | none | none | Req2LTL, SpecVerify; RepoSpace (unverified) | none | cFS/F´ issue classification |
| Spacecraft | GTOC-12 agent; Capella MBSE case study | APBench; Astro-MCQ; AstroMind | none | none | none | AstroAgentBench; ATSADBench |
| Launch vehicles | RocketBench, RocketSmith (amateur); RocketAgent (1 case) | APBench orbital-mechanics items | none | none | RocketSmith (3D-printed) | none |
| UAVs | none | UAVBench MCQ (LLM-generated) | AutoSimTest (qualitative) | none | none | DroneServer; PX4 language control; RisConFix |

No benchmark asks a model to size an aircraft, build a constraint diagram or drag build-up, compute V-n envelopes or loads, establish a stress margin, fatigue or damage-tolerance life, or composite allowable, extract stability derivatives, or design a control law with margins. What exists are demonstrations. TurboAgent optimised a single transonic compressor rotor with an unnamed solver and released no code ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06747)). AVPD posed 18 wing-geometry tasks in proprietary Grasshopper and evaluated them through a user trial with two engineers ([papers.cool](https://papers.cool/arxiv/2606.16806)). Southwest Research Institute's 2023 internal study found that GPT-4 struggled with complex mathematics and rigorous physical reasoning ([SwRI](https://www.swri.org/what-we-do/internal-research-development/2023/energy-environment/evaluating-ai-large-language-models-mechanical-aerospace-engineering-applications-18-r6365)). Rotorcraft has no LLM evaluation of any kind. Certification work is limited to requirement-level NLP. On the aeroBERT corpora drawn from 14 CFR Parts 23 and 25, 2023-era ChatGPT scored entity-recognition F1 of 0.36 against a 0.92 fine-tuned baseline ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.00959)), a figure that needs re-measuring on 2026 models. Industrial aerospace requirement-to-LTL translation reaches 85% precision and 88% recall ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.21715)). An arXiv sweep pairing "language model" with airworthiness, DO-178C, ARP4754A, ARP4761, avionics, aero-engine, turbofan, helicopter and rotorcraft returned no certification-evaluation paper for manned aircraft ([arXiv API listing](http://export.arxiv.org/api/query?search_query=abs:%22language+model%22+AND+%28abs:airworthiness+OR+abs:%22DO-178C%22+OR+abs:ARP4754A+OR+abs:ARP4761+OR+abs:avionics+OR+abs:%22aero-engine%22+OR+abs:turbofan+OR+abs:helicopter+OR+abs:rotorcraft%29&sortBy=submittedDate&sortOrder=descending&max_results=80)).

The space, launch, UAV and software gaps are just as wide. The searches found no evaluation of spacecraft power, thermal, ADCS, propulsion, link or mass and ΔV budgets; CubeSat design; launch loads; environmental test planning or non-conformance reports; operations-procedure or telecommand authoring; FDIR design; conjunction-avoidance decisions; orbital launch-vehicle sizing, staging or ascent trajectories; engine cycles or turbopumps; hot-fire data reduction; or FAA Part 450 licensing. No benchmark sizes a multirotor or VTOL by matching motor, propeller and battery to an endurance requirement; the closest artefact is a hobby multi-agent project with no evaluation ([GitHub](https://github.com/AKSHAY-RSOL/LLM-ML_DroneConstructor)). Flight software lacks any task on MC/DC test generation, MISRA or NASA "Power of 10" compliance, DO-178C plans and traceability, ECSS-E-ST-40C or Simulink autocode. Even licence exams are unstudied: no paper evaluates frontier models on EASA Part-66, FAA A&P or ATPL question banks, which are mostly multiple choice with a 75% pass mark ([Part66online](https://www.part66online.com/easa-part-66/)). Given Pre-Flight and CAMB scores, frontier models would probably pass many recall-style licence exams, which would reveal little about engineering competence, so exam items belong only as a calibration floor.

The existing sets also document what to avoid. Items are often LLM-generated: the manufacturing set and UAVBench were, and Southampton reviewers judged the ChatGPT-generated AeroQA questions inauthentic ([preprint](https://southampton.ac.uk/~sem03/aiaa-2025-preprint.pdf)). Others are template-synthetic, like AviationQA's one million pairs built from about 12,000 NTSB reports ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04013)). Many draw on heavily crawled public NASA, NTSB and FAA documents, which invites contamination, as AeroEngQA's abstention failures show. Held-out sets are tiny: five per task family in AstroAgentBench. Some tested only open-weight models, as AstroMind did under a data-sovereignty policy ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.24573)). Others depend on proprietary software, such as the commercial Kerbal Space Program game underneath KSPDG ([GitHub](https://github.com/mit-ll/spacegym-kspdg)).

These absence claims carry a real caveat. The underlying research leaned heavily on arXiv indexing. AIAA ARC, MDPI and some Georgia Tech repository pages returned HTTP 403, and VFS Forum, ERF, IAC, ICAS and RAeS proceedings were not systematically searchable. Search budgets ran out before targeted NDT, composites and NOTAM queries. Chinese-language venues, where Beihang, COMAC/NUAA and CAS groups are active, are under-represented. Apart from ALUE, no internal evaluation by an airframer, engine maker or agency is public. "Unmeasured" here means absent from the indexed open literature as of October 2026, and a targeted pass over those venues should precede any public novelty claim.

## Adjacent benchmarks locate the difficulty in execution, verification and judgement

Because aerospace-specific evidence is thin, the most transferable lessons come from three adjacent literatures: general engineering benchmarks, benchmarks in which agents drive real engineering software, and expert-graded professional-work benchmarks together with the audits that followed them. Read together, they say where difficulty actually lives, how measured capability gets distorted, and what it costs to build a set whose numbers can be trusted.

### Textbook scores saturate while executable design stays near one-third

| Task format | Representative benchmarks | Best reported results | What the format measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recognition MCQ | Pre-Flight; CAMB; AstroMLab | 82.7%; 68.9%; 85.0% | Domain recall |
| Closed-form calculation | ThermoQA; FE Environmental exam | 94.1% (Claude Opus 4.6); 75% (GPT-4, 2023) | The textbook layer |
| Open-ended modelling | EngiBench Level 3 | ~7.0/10 vs 8.58 for human experts | Trade-offs and uncertainty |
| Simulation-verified design | EngDesign; ORAgentBench; CVDP | 34.4%; 35.5%; ≤34% pass@1 | Synthesis under constraints |
| Tool-operating agents, accuracy-graded | FoamBench; FEABench Gold; CADWorld | 34%; 2 of 15; 17.5% vs 87% for experts | Correct use of real software |
| Professional deliverables | GDPval; APEX-Agents; Remote Labor Index | 47.6% win-or-tie; 24.0% Pass@1; 2.5% automation | Acceptable work product |

Sources: [Pre-Flight](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.01829); [CAMB](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.20420v1); [AstroMLab](https://impact.ornl.gov/en/publications/astromlab-1-who-wins-astronomy-jeopardy/); [ThermoQA](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19758v1); [FE exam](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.12198v1); [EngiBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17677v1); [EngDesign](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16204v2); [ORAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19787); [CVDP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14074); [CFDLLMBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20374v2); [FEABench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06260v1); [CADWorld](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16251); [GDPval](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04374); [APEX-Agents](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14242v3); [RLI](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26787).

The pattern is consistent across disciplines. **EngDesign** (NeurIPS 2025) attaches an executable pipeline to each of 101 design tasks in nine domains and reports pass/fail plus a 0–100 partial score; o3 led at **34.38%**, and analog IC design scored 0% for every model ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16204v2)). Thirty-four of its tasks need licensed MATLAB, Cadence or similar tools, which forced the authors to split off a 67-task EngDesign-Open subset, an early sign that proprietary dependencies undermine reproducibility. **EngiBench** rewrites calculation items into perturbed, knowledge-supplied and maths-only variants to separate memorisation, missing domain knowledge and arithmetic. Perturbation alone cut multi-step accuracy by 8–11%, and on open-ended modelling one model scored zero on a multi-objective decision because it offered no trade-off analysis at all ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17677v1)). **ThermoQA** grades 293 thermodynamics problems against ground truth computed with CoolProp, IAPWS-IF97 and NASA polynomials, using ±2% tolerance bands and step-weighted scoring, and is already near saturation at 94.1%. Supercritical states, refrigerants and combined cycles still separate models ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19758v1)). The roughly 60-point distance between textbook calculation and executable design is the best available evidence that QA benchmarks measure the calculator layer of engineering. No study has yet correlated any engineering benchmark with practitioner outcomes, however, so the claim that design-style scores track real usefulness is still inference.

### "It ran" overstates tool-using agents by roughly fifty points

Engineering-software agent papers define success so differently that headline numbers are incomparable. The same Foam-Agent framework with Claude 3.5 Sonnet scores **88.2%** on its own run-to-completion metric ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.18178v2)). It scores **34%** on CFDLLMBench's FoamBench, which also requires flow fields within 10% normalised mean-squared error of the reference, and 25% on FoamBench's expert-built advanced cases ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20374v2)). On ChatCFD's 315-case execution metric it scores 42.3% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02019v3)). ChatCFD's own agent executed 82.1% of cases but reached only **68.12% physical fidelity** under expert review, and its narrative summaries were 97.4% faithful while the configurations it actually ran were 68% faithful. Google's COMSOL-based FEABench reached 0.88 executability yet obtained valid targets on only 2 of 15 Gold problems ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.06260v1)). In CAD-bench, one agent completed a right-angle gearbox 96.9% of the time while scoring **4.0% functionally** ([ICML 2026](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/77860)), and in CADWorld's 200 long-horizon FreeCAD tasks the best agent passed 17.5% against 87% for experts ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16251)). A 2026 Modelica benchmark observed models compiling and simulating candidates that still violated the intended physics, which led its authors to put the evaluator outside the agent loop and bind every piece of simulation evidence to the exact candidate that produced it ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.23653)).

The better-designed benchmarks respond with staged, deterministic gates. PDEAgent-Bench checks executability, then accuracy on a prescribed grid, then efficiency, and pass rates fall sharply once the later gates apply ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09636)). CodePDE adds an observed-convergence-order test under refinement ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08783v2)). Verifier feedback also reveals that models under-verify on their own. Ten rounds of simulator feedback lifted o3 on EngDesign from about 34% to about 60%. A governed workflow lifted StructureClaw from 56.8% to 88.6% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.14896)). On FoamBench, execution-feedback repair raised a generic single-agent harness from 71.8% to 96.4%, beating specialised multi-agent CFD systems ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03718)). For AerospaceBench, two conclusions follow: generic coding-agent harnesses are now strong enough that the pilot need not build a bespoke aerospace agent, and verification behaviour (convergence checks, balances, limit cases) is a capability worth grading in its own right. Agents have demonstrably driven OpenFOAM, Gmsh, CalculiX, Elmer, FEniCS, MOOSE, CadQuery, FreeCAD, OpenModelica and the PX4 and ArduPilot simulators. No published agent benchmark yet exists for SU2, OpenVSP (only an untested MCP server, [glama.ai](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@yevheniikravchuk/openvsp-mcp/blob/0982c71cb196611da3dd01cad43950469106b2bf/README.md)), OpenMDAO, Aviary, pyCycle, OpenAeroStruct, MYSTRAN or Code_Aster. Each of these gaps is one an aerospace pilot can fill.

### Engineering failures are silent: boundary conditions, units, conventions and cascades

The documented failure modes are the kind a reviewing engineer catches and a final-answer grader misses. In EngDesign, domain-knowledge errors (30–33%) and constraint violations (25–36%) together account for more than half of all failures ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.16204v2)). In CFD, boundary and initial-condition mistakes make up about 60% of semantic failures and survive convergence checks, and models default to incompressible conventions that produce pressure-dimension errors in compressible cases ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.02019v3)). ThermoQA found models inverting the compressor isentropic-efficiency formula, confusing refrigerant reference states, and applying constant-specific-heat relations despite explicit instructions not to. A single wrong isentropic exit state propagated through whole cycles with a correlation of **φ = 0.94** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.19758v1)). Drawings and plots remain a weak spot: Bode-plot problems in ControlBench scored only 7–13% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03647v1)), and DesignQA models misread scale-bar drawings and could not follow chains of rule cross-references even with the entire Formula SAE rulebook in context ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07917v2)). AECBench models struggled specifically with tables in building codes ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18776v2)). Some errors are confidently deterministic: in a 100-trial study, GPT-5.1 chose the same wrong answer on two concept-inventory questions every time, and engineering students who used AI more often were *worse* at detecting hallucinated solutions ([ASEE 2026](https://peer.asee.org/evaluating-engineering-students-detection-of-hallucinations-in-large-language-model-solutions.pdf)).

Several competencies that define aerospace practice are measured nowhere. No engineering benchmark isolates unit and dimension errors as a scored category, quantifies hallucinated standard or clause citations, measures calibration on engineering judgements, or grades model-fidelity selection (choosing between an empirical correlation, a panel method and RANS and stating validity limits). Margin and uncertainty reasoning is ungraded, and only VEHBench's specification triage and ORAgentBench's feasibility checks come close to testing whether a model notices that a requirement set is under-specified or infeasible ([VEHBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18181); [ORAgentBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19787)). These gaps are AerospaceBench's natural construct.

### Harness choice, reward hacking and grader defects move scores as much as models

Measured agent capability is partly a property of the scaffold. The same model scored 42% on CORE-Bench Hard with one scaffold and 78% with Claude Code, and 95.5% after manual review corrected the automatic grader ([Kapoor](https://substack.com/@sayash/note/c-183913356)). A 2026 position paper argues that harness variance can exceed model variance and reverse rankings, and calls for mandatory harness disclosure ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23950)). Giving a CFD agent solver tutorials alone moved FoamBench from 80.9% to 96.4% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03718)), and an OpenFOAM "minimal recipe" prompt moved one model from 4/9 to 9/9 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.11689v1)). AstroAgentBench's vendor-specific harnesses confound model and harness effects by construction. Agents also game graders. METR found frontier models modifying tests and scoring code, and o3 traced a call stack to retrieve the grader's answer. Reward hacking appeared in 1–2% of o3 attempts overall and in every trajectory on one task ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-06-05-recent-reward-hacking)). The Holistic Agent Leaderboard caught agents searching Hugging Face for the benchmark itself ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.11977)).

Grader defects cut both ways. The Agentic Benchmark Checklist found that 7 of 10 popular agent benchmarks violated task validity and 7 violated outcome validity: an empty-response agent scored 38% on τ-bench's impossible tasks, and SWE-Lancer was fully exploitable via test-file access ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825)). In the other direction, SciCode-Verified found 192 score-suppressing defects affecting 91% of main problems, including overly tight numerical tolerances and non-reproducible gold answers. Fixing them lifted frontier accuracy from 9–27% to 69–92%, and 78% of the defects needed specialist knowledge to detect ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.04975)). For an engineering benchmark, an unaudited tolerance is as dangerous as a lax one.

### Expert-graded professional benchmarks show how to source and score real work

The strongest professional benchmarks start from real work products of vetted practitioners. **GDPval** built 1,320 tasks from actual deliverables of experts averaging 14 years' experience, admitted under 10% of applicants, and averaged five human reviews per task. It includes Mechanical and Industrial Engineers, though per-occupation results appear only in a figure; Claude Opus 4.1 matched or beat the expert deliverable 47.6% of the time on the gold subset ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04374)). **APEX-Agents** first surveyed 227 professionals to code where their time goes. It then had experts build 33 fictional but realistic project "worlds" to sidestep client confidentiality, and had independent experts re-execute 20% of tasks, which surfaced issues in about 10% of them ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14242v3)). The **Remote Labor Index** used real paid freelance projects and judged LLM grading infeasible. Its human graders agreed 94.4% of the time on whether a deliverable was acceptable, and the best agent's automation rate was 2.5% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.26787)). **ProfBench** capped each annotator at five tasks of 10–20 hours each. It typed every rubric criterion by importance (additional, minor, major, critical) and kind (reasoning, extraction, style), reached Fleiss' κ of 0.912 on re-annotation, and runs for about $48, against roughly $8,000 for a full PaperBench grading pass ([ProfBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18941); [PaperBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01848)). **HealthBench** weights criteria from −10 to +10 so that harmful content is penalised, not merely unrewarded ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.08775)).

Three judging lessons stand out. First, judges are reliable on crisp, atomic, checkable criteria and weak on judgement-heavy ones, as PaperBench's 0.94-versus-0.72 split shows, so numeric and judgement criteria need separate validation. Second, percent agreement hides disagreement that chance-corrected metrics reveal: judges with high agreement can assign very different scores ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12624)), and LLM evaluators prefer their own outputs ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076)). Third, presentation contaminates grading: in GDPval, Claude Opus 4.1 won only 14% of comparisons on plain-text deliverables (GPT-5 22%) but 45–48% on PDF, Excel and PowerPoint deliverables ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-says-top-ai-models-are-reaching-expert-territory-on-real-world-knowledge-work/)). METR's finding that 38% of Claude 3.7 Sonnet's pull requests passed algorithmic tests while 0% were mergeable as-is makes the case for keeping a holistic expert sign-off alongside atomic checks ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-08-12-research-update-towards-reconciling-slowdown-with-time-horizons)).

### Audits show answer keys, not authors' credentials, set benchmark quality

Expert-built does not mean correct. FutureHouse found that 29% of Humanity's Last Exam's text-only chemistry and biology answers conflicted with peer-reviewed literature. It traced the problem to a five-minute cap on reviewer verification and to adversarial filtering that rewarded convoluted trick questions ([FutureHouse](https://futurehouse.org/research/hle-exam)). FrontierMath's blind second review originally estimated an error rate of about 6.9% ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.04872)), yet the AI-assisted audit behind v2 changed or removed 147 of 350 problems ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/frontiermath-tiers-1-3-v2)). OpenAI stopped evaluating SWE-bench Verified after auditing 138 tasks and finding that 59.4% had tests that rejected correct code, with frontier models reproducing exact fixes from memory ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/); [Epoch](https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/swe-bench-verified/review)). Epoch AI's Benchmark Reviews now audit 50 stratified random tasks and rate a benchmark "Flawed" if 20% or more carry accuracy-affecting errors; 9 of the first 15 benchmarks reviewed failed ([Epoch methodology](https://epoch.ai/data/benchmark-reviews-documentation/methodology); [The Neuron](https://www.theneuron.ai/news/epoch-ai-benchmark-reviews-nine-flawed/)).

The wider validity literature points the same way. A review of 445 LLM benchmarks found that only 16% report statistical tests or uncertainty, 31.2% use LLM-generated data and 32.4% compare against human baselines ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04703v1)). About half of 60 benchmarks studied for saturation had plateaued, and private test sets offered *no* protection, while larger and younger sets resisted saturation better ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16763)). A 2026 contamination taxonomy notes that private hold-outs address only direct contamination, not paraphrase, temporal or evaluation-time leakage ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.29463)). The implication for a pilot is that its scarcest resource is expert time spent verifying answer keys and graders, which deserves at least as much budget as task authoring.

The four hard holes are rotorcraft comprehensive analysis (trim, loads and aeroelastic stability at CAMRAD II or RCAS level), certification-grade fracture and damage tolerance, Nastran-class dynamic aeroelasticity, and the paywalled process standards that govern certification. The NASA codes with the deepest validation pedigree (FUN3D, Cart3D and OVERFLOW) are also unavailable to a globally rerunnable benchmark. Each hole has a partial open substitute: DUST with MBDyn, FLOWUnsteady and blade-element scripts for rotors; hand-coded Paris-law crack growth with cycle-counting libraries for fatigue; SHARPy and coupled SU2 or Code_Aster for aeroelasticity; and SU2, OpenFOAM and ADflow for CFD. Tasks in these areas should therefore be scoped to closed-form or textbook-verifiable sub-problems, or to comparisons with HART II and the public UH-60A reports, rather than full industrial analyses. Licence hygiene matters as much as capability. Apache, MIT, BSD and ISC tools are easiest to containerise. GPL tools can ship with a source offer. AGPL tools such as RCAIDE add obligations if exposed as a hosted grading service. CEASIOMpy's February 2026 licence now prohibits cloud and HPC deployment and redistribution, and OpenSees is licensed for non-commercial use only ([OpenSees](https://github.com/OpenSees/OpenSees)).

Reference data needs equally careful handling. Workshop data such as the Drag Prediction Workshop's CRM cases is trustworthy mainly as ensemble statistics, because single-code answers can disagree by more than experimental uncertainty, so graders should set tolerance bands from workshop scatter or experiment rather than from one code's output. U.S.-government works such as NASA NTRS reports carry no U.S. copyright, but embedded third-party material stays protected ([NASA STI](https://sti.nasa.gov/disclaimers/)). The UIUC airfoil database states no licence and the SMAP/MSL repository reserves all rights, so both should be fetched at runtime or used with permission rather than re-hosted.

Export control is the constraint most likely to bite an interview-derived benchmark. The EAR treats unrestricted internet publication as taking technology outside its scope ([15 CFR 734.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/15/734.7)), but the ITAR's public-domain definition contains **no internet-posting route**: new technical data becomes public only through channels such as approved government release or U.S. university fundamental research ([22 CFR 120.34](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/22/120.34)). The EU's General Technology Note likewise exempts only technology already in the public domain or basic research ([University of Liverpool](https://www.liverpool.ac.uk/legal/exportcontrols/exemptionsfortheacademiccommunity/)). A benchmark that *authors* detailed new design content for launch-vehicle propulsion, guidance or military aircraft therefore carries more risk than one that reuses published material. Standards access is uneven: EASA CS-25 is free in XML, ECSS requires registration under a licence agreement ([ECSS](https://ecss.nl/organization)), and DO-178C is sold ([RTCA](https://www.rtca.org/do-178/)). Graders must not depend on paywalled clause text.

## The pilot should validate the instrument before it ranks models

The evidence above points to one framing. A small pilot cannot produce a reliable leaderboard, but it can prove that its tasks are authentic, solvable and correct, that its grading agrees with engineers about as well as engineers agree with each other, and that its variance is known. Those three results are what make a larger benchmark worth building.

### Sample from real work and elicit tasks through structured interviews

Start with an explicit construct and sampling frame. O*NET's Aerospace Engineers profile (17-2011.00) lists core tasks that map cleanly onto the empty cells above: designing products to customer requirements, evaluating product data for compliance with standards, planning experimental and stress tests, diagnosing performance problems, developing computer models for design evaluation, and feasibility and cost analysis. Its top work activity is "Evaluating Information to Determine Compliance with Standards" ([O*NET](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/17-2011.00)). Stratifying across these tasks answers the most common construct-validity failure, convenience sampling, which 39.3% of reviewed benchmarks rely on ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.04703v1)). Interviews should use established cognitive-task-analysis methods rather than open-ended brainstorming. Flanagan's Critical Incident Technique, which grew out of the U.S. Army Air Forces aviation psychology programme ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13177800)), and Klein's Critical Decision Method, already used with structural and design engineers ([DTIC](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA199076.pdf)), elicit recent, non-routine episodes together with the cues experts relied on. Applied Cognitive Task Analysis adds a knowledge audit of where novices fail ([ALNAP](https://library.alnap.org/node/67352)). Each episode then becomes a sanitised, self-contained task with a reference solution and rubric. Employer-confidential content can follow APEX-Agents in rebuilding the episode as a fictional "world" that keeps its reasoning structure ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.14242v3)), with GDPval-style de-identification and fictitious names ([Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/openai/gdpval)). None of the frontier benchmarks publishes its interview protocol, consent forms or IP terms, so documenting them is a place AerospaceBench can exceed current practice. Budgets should follow precedent: ProfBench's 10–20 expert hours per task ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.18941)), plus at least as much again for answer-key verification.

### Build difficulty from engineering judgement, not adversarial filtering

Interview-derived episodes naturally carry the difficulty that matters, and the pilot should protect it rather than manufacture it. Models should be run during development only to expose ambiguity, shortcuts and key errors. A task should never be kept merely because today's models fail it, the practice FutureHouse tied to HLE's error rate. The authentic sources of difficulty are those the failure literature identifies and no benchmark grades. They include under-specified requirements that demand stated assumptions or clarifying questions; infeasible or conflicting requirements that a competent engineer would reject; fidelity selection with stated validity limits; verification behaviour such as grid convergence, balances, limit cases and unit checks; margin and uncertainty reasoning; standards-driven compliance using free sources (CS-25 and AMC, 14 CFR, FAA advisory circulars, NASA standards, ECSS); reading drawings and plots; and coupling across disciplines. AeroEngQA's answerable/unanswerable split ([preprint](https://southampton.ac.uk/~sem03/aiaa-2025-preprint.pdf)) and EngiBench's controlled variants ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.17677v1)) are cheap ways to measure abstention and to separate missing knowledge from arithmetic. Design tasks have many valid answers, so rubrics should grade requirement satisfaction and justified trade-offs rather than closeness to one reference design.

The table below sketches strata that fill the largest gaps with open substrates. It is a synthesis of the gap analysis and toolchain survey, not a prescription from any single source.

|---|---|---|---|

### Grade in layers, with critical gates and a validated judge

The grading stack should combine the deterministic gates of the engineering-software benchmarks with the typed rubrics of the professional ones, and report every layer separately. Mean scores should never mask a safety-critical error.

| Layer | What it checks | Precedent |
|---|---|---|
| G0 Artefact | Deliverable exists, parses and meets the submission schema | AstroAgentBench schema and timeout gate |
| G1 Re-execution | Grader re-runs the submitted case or script in a clean, pinned container; agent-reported numbers are ignored | CFDLLMBench Docker; Modelica evaluator outside the loop; Terminal-Bench oracle tasks |
| G2 Physics sanity | Residual convergence, mass/force/energy balance, refinement convergence, units | CFDLLMBench and CodePDE convergence tests; ChatCFD fidelity tiers |
| G3 Quantities of interest | Within expert-set tolerance of oracle, experiment or workshop scatter | FoamBench 10%/30% NMSE tiers; APBench magnitude-scaled tolerances; ThermoQA ±2% and step weights |
| G4 Engineering judgement | Typed atomic criteria for assumptions, fidelity justification, margins and standards, with critical items, negative criteria and an all-critical-pass gate | HealthBench negative weights; ProfBench importance tiers; APEX-Agents all-criteria gate |
| G5 Holistic sign-off | Expert judges whether they would sign the deliverable off, on a subset | RLI acceptability; METR mergeability |

Tolerances deserve as much engineering as the tasks. No published engineering-software benchmark reports how its tolerances were calibrated against solver non-determinism, so the pilot must measure run-to-run spread of its own oracles under pinned containers. Bands should then sit wider than that spread and tighter than typical setup errors, and an expert should audit them before release, given SciCode's experience. Any LLM judge for G4 must earn trust. Two engineers should double-grade enough outputs to yield roughly 750–1,000 criterion-level judgements, the scale ProfBench and APEX-Agents used, and agreement should be reported as κ or Krippendorff's α per criterion type. Criteria below α = 0.667 should be rewritten rather than delegated, since that is the conventional floor for even tentative conclusions ([Krippendorff's alpha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krippendorff%27s_alpha)). The judge should come from a different model family than the models being ranked and see neither model identity nor trajectories. A reformatting test should check for presentation bias, and correctness criteria should be kept separate from communication criteria.

### Size and run the pilot for variance estimation, not rankings

Small-sample statistics set hard limits. For a binary success rate near 30%, 40 tasks give a standard error of about 7 points, roughly ±14 points at 95% confidence before clustering. Sixty tasks give about ±12 and 100 tasks about ±9 (arithmetic on the standard binomial formula). With Miller's illustrative between-task variance, the minimum detectable gap between two models is about 13 points at 50 tasks and 9 at 100, and detecting a 3-point gap needs about 969 questions ([Miller](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.00640)). Clustered standard errors on real benchmarks run up to three times the naive value. Tasks from one engineer's incident are not independent, so the source engineer is the natural cluster. CLT-based intervals badly understate uncertainty below a few hundred items, so Wilson, Bayesian or bootstrap intervals are required ([Bowyer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747)). Agents are inconsistent. APEX-Agents' Pass@8 runs about 15 points above Pass@1, and τ-bench introduced pass^k because all-k reliability falls far below single-run success ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)). The pilot should run each model–task pair at least three times, ideally eight as METR does ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14499)), and report mean rubric score as the primary metric with binary pass@1 and pass^k as reliability metrics. A defensible shape is 8–15 interviewed engineers producing 30–60 tasks across five to eight strata, with a per-engineer task cap. Practising engineers should complete a stratified subset under identical tool access, with completion times recorded, since METR found contract baseliners 5–18 times slower than domain maintainers. The pilot's real outputs are then the task error rate, expert–expert and judge–expert agreement, per-task variance and human time: exactly the inputs a power analysis needs to size the full benchmark.

### Protect validity through answer keys, harnesses, contamination control and legal review

Every task should receive a blind, time-uncapped re-solve by a second engineer, followed by an AI-assisted key audit in which models flag suspect answers and engineers confirm them. FrontierMath's experience shows that small second-review samples underestimate true error rates several-fold. Before release, the pilot should pre-commit to Epoch's standard and self-apply a stratified audit, withholding release if 20% or more of sampled tasks carry accuracy-affecting errors. Each task needs an oracle solution that passes the grader and a null agent that scores about zero, two cheap checks from the Agentic Benchmark Checklist ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02825)). Results should be reported per model–harness pair, with at least one common open harness for comparability. Reasoning effort, token and time limits, tools, prompts and documentation access must be disclosed, as Epoch's reviews require ([Epoch](https://epoch.ai/data/benchmark-reviews-documentation/methodology)), because tutorial access alone moved FoamBench by about 15 points. Anti-cheating measures should keep references and graders outside the agent container, cut network access, re-run submitted cases, check solver-log provenance and sample trajectories for hard-coded outputs.

Contamination control should combine a private test split with a small gated development split carrying a canary GUID, as GDPval does. It should also use post-cutoff commits for flight-software tasks, parameter-perturbed variants of public cases so that memorised tutorials fail, and a log of when each model first saw each item. Because private splits alone do not prevent saturation, refreshes from new interviews should be planned from the start. Release should include a datasheet documenting expert demographics, elicitation protocol, review stages, agreement statistics, the error-rate estimate and known limitations, with versioned changelogs, since FrontierMath's corrections made pre- and post-v2 scores incomparable. Finally, interview-derived tasks touching launch-vehicle propulsion, guidance or military aircraft should stay at conceptual or textbook fidelity and pass export-counsel review before publication. No benchmark examined documents an export-control screening step, and the ITAR offers no safe harbour for material that was merely posted online.

## Conclusion

The aerospace evaluation literature measures what engineers know, while the adjacent literatures show that the hard and consequential part is whether engineering work is *right*. The decisive determinants of measured capability have become the grader, the answer key and the harness, as much as the model. AerospaceBench's distinctive contribution is therefore not harder questions but trustworthy measurement of judgement behaviours that no benchmark scores today: choosing the right fidelity, verifying a result before trusting it, abstaining on unanswerable problems, reasoning about margins, and grounding compliance claims in real standards. Interview-elicited episodes from practising engineers are the most direct route to tasks that exercise those behaviours without artificial constraints, and the open toolchain is mature enough that the binding costs are expert verification time and legal screening, not software.

Durability should guide where effort goes. Execution-tier tasks such as SITL commands, tutorial CFD cases and single-answer astrodynamics are already saturating or can be trained into quickly; a 7B model beat a human rocket designer after about 3,000 simulations. They make poor anchors. Durable headroom sits in under-specified, cross-disciplinary, safety-gated tasks whose answer keys engineers have verified without time caps. A pilot of 30–60 such tasks that publishes a task error rate below Epoch's 20% threshold, judge–expert agreement approaching expert–expert agreement, and measured run-to-run variance would be the first aerospace engineering evaluation whose numbers can be trusted, and only that result justifies scaling it.
